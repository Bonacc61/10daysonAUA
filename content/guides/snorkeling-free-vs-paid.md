# Snorkeling in Aruba: when the free beach beats the boat trip

**The short version: if you want to snorkel with green turtles, you do not need
to book anything.** Walk down the steps at Tres Trapi before nine in the
morning, float still, and wait. It costs the price of a mask.

That is not the advice you will get from someone selling a $65 catamaran ticket,
and it is not universally true either — there are three specific cases where
paying is clearly the better call, and we say which below. But the default
assumption most visitors arrive with, that snorkeling is a thing you book, is
wrong on this island.

Aruba's leeward coast is unusually kind to shore snorkelers. The water is calm,
shallow and clear, the reef sits close in, and the turtles graze seagrass within
swimming distance of a parking spot.

---

## The free spots, and what each is actually for

**Tres Trapi — for turtles.** Three concrete steps drop you straight into a
sheltered cove where green turtles feed on the seagrass. No entry fee, no boat,
no booking. Free plus about $16 for gear rental.

> "Walk down the steps slowly, float, and wait. They surface for air right
> beside you." — Glennis

**Malmok — for reef fish and the best clarity.** Calm, shallow water over a
rocky bottom, with the reef close enough to swim to. The Antilla wreck lies
offshore from here, though not within a comfortable shore swim.

> "Get in before the catamarans arrive around 10 and the turtles are all yours."
> — Edsel

That timing advice is worth taking literally. Checked against the operators'
own published schedules, the large catamarans depart between **09:00 and
09:30** — so Edsel's "around 10" is exactly when the first boats reach the
snorkel stops. Arrive at eight and you have the water to yourself. Arrive at
half ten and you are sharing it with several hundred people.

**Boca Catalina — the same reef, from the road.** A small rocky cove between
Malmok and Arashi where the catamarans anchor offshore. Walk in from the road,
swim out twenty metres, and you are in the same water the boats brought their
passengers to see. Car needed.

**Mangel Halto — for mangroves, with a real caveat.** A mangrove-fringed lagoon
on the calm south coast. Wade in from the dock, and past the mangroves the reef
drops into a wall of fish. This is the most interesting free snorkel on the
island and the only one that comes with a warning:

> "Snorkel out through the cut in the reef — but only on a calm day, and always
> head back before the current turns." — Orlando

**Baby Beach — for weak swimmers and small children.** A shallow, bath-warm
lagoon on the southern tip, effectively a natural nursery for tropical fish.
Nothing here will frighten anybody.

> "Drive past the refinery and keep going. Bath-warm and crystal clear."
> — Miguel

## When paying is genuinely the better call

**1. You want the Antilla wreck.** The 400-foot WWII freighter is the single
best thing to snorkel in Aruba, and it is not reachable from shore. You can see
the whole hull from the surface in clear water — but you need a boat to get
above it. This is the strongest reason on the list.

**2. You have no car.** Tres Trapi, Boca Catalina, Mangel Halto and Baby Beach
all need one. If you are staying on Palm Beach without wheels, a catamaran that
collects you is not an indulgence, it is the only practical route to a reef.

**3. You want turtles guaranteed, in ninety minutes, with photos.** Shore
snorkeling trades certainty for cost. The small private turtle tours invert
that: a guide who knows where the turtles are today, in and out inside two
hours. The best-rated of them departs at **06:30** — before the catamaran fleet,
and before the wind picks up.

A fourth reason people rarely admit to: the big catamarans are a **social day
out** with an open bar and music, and the snorkeling is a stop on it. That is a
perfectly good thing to want. Just buy it knowing that is what you are buying.

Two things stand out.

**The private turtle tours are rated in a different league.** 99% and 96%
five-star against 81–90% for the big boats. Small groups, short trips, guides
who find turtles. If turtles are the point and the budget stretches, that is

**And the one to think hardest about: Small Group Snorkeling at Mangel Halto,

the cut in the reef and the turning current — this is the one free spot where a
guide is buying you judgement, not access. If you are an uncertain swimmer, that
$55 is defensible. If you are confident and the day is calm, it is not.

## Our pick

**Go to Tres Trapi at eight in the morning with rented gear, and spend the money
you saved on one boat trip to the Antilla wreck.**

That gets you the two genuinely distinct experiences — turtles at close range in
quiet water, and a shipwreck you cannot otherwise reach — for roughly the price
of one catamaran ticket for two people. Skip the second boat trip. The reef stop
on most catamaran itineraries is Boca Catalina, and you can swim to it from the
road.

## Common questions

**Do I need a boat to snorkel with turtles in Aruba?**
No. Tres Trapi and Malmok both have green turtles reachable from shore, free.
Go before 9am, before the catamarans arrive at their snorkel stops.

**What does snorkel gear cost if I don't bring my own?**
About $16 to rent for the day near the main beaches — far less than any trip.

**Which free snorkel spot is best for children?**
Baby Beach. Shallow, calm and bath-warm, with tropical fish and no current.

**Can I snorkel the Antilla shipwreck from the beach?**
Not practically. It lies offshore from Malmok and needs a boat, which is the
clearest reason to pay for a trip.

**What time do the catamarans reach the snorkel stops?**
They depart between 09:00 and 09:30, so the popular spots get busy from about
10am. Before that you will often have the water to yourself.

**Is a guided trip worth it at Mangel Halto when it's free to enter?**
Only for the judgement. The route out through the cut in the reef depends on
conditions and the current turns — a guide is buying you that call, not access.
